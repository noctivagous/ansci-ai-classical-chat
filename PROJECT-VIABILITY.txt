
are we really going to be able to put together a viable chat bot by gathering these different graphs and projects together?  are we really going to get the linguistics coverage?

The honest answer splits your question in two, because they're different claims.
Linguistics coverage — yes, really, and it's the measurable part. That claim isn't faith; it's counted. WordNet's sense inventory, VerbNet's 4,135 verbs, Link Grammar's 59,291 forms covering 76.1% of derivable verb lemmas — each resource's coverage is a number computed from the actual files, and the parser-compare set plus the sealed held-out sets measure what survives end to end. Where coverage stops is also known and located: fragments throw, relatives drop until the routing lands, the Mandarin side has no equivalent dictionary. Coverage assembled this way fails by name — you can point at the missing link family or the absent lemma. That is a genuinely strong property, and no hand-built lexicon was ever going to reach it.
A viable chatbot — that's a different claim, and the graphs don't make it. Coverage is about sentences. A conversation is about turns: accumulating what the person means across exchanges, knowing who said what, asking a clarifying question when the gap is real, declining at the located gap instead of bluffing, remembering the person. Almost none of that lives in WordNet or Link Grammar. It lives in the parts of AnSci that are least proven — the intent arc, the Interlocutor picture, the Social Unit — which is exactly why your ladder exists and why it honestly reads "0 Demonstrator (today)," with Rung 1's bar a checklist that hasn't been passed yet.
So, stepped back:
- What has been shown: the assembled resources parse and check a bounded slice of English with located failures, and each adoption (SymSpell, the dictionary slice) measurably widened that slice without hand-writing the widening.
- What has not been shown: that the whole pipeline holds on real conversational material — nobody has yet run the number that actually decides it, which is what fraction of ordinary chat turns survive token → parse → roles → senses → graph → a reply that doesn't misattribute or flatten. The challenge sets point at this; a full end-to-end score on real dialogue doesn't exist yet.
- What nobody independent has checked: all of it. Every gate tonight is internal — our tests, our sealed sets, our comparisons. The sealed-set discipline guards against self-deception in one direction (tuning to the exam), but the exam is still ours.
My actual judgment: this assembly strategy is a sound route to Rungs 0–2 — a demonstrator that becomes a presence: bounded, inspectable, honest about its gaps, correct where it's correct in a way a neural chatbot isn't. It is not, by itself, a route to open-domain fluency, and no pile of graphs changes that — Rung 5 would need the discourse layer to be as real as the lexical layer now is, and that layer is currently design and partial build, not evidence.
The doubt is well-placed if "viable" means fluent. If "viable" means a system that converses within a known territory, says what it can't take and where, and widens by measured slices — that, the evidence so far supports building toward, one gated rung at a time.
has anyone attempted this kind of project?
"The doubt is well-placed if "viable" means fluent”  what are the reasons?

Yes — and the strongest evidence is that this exact assembly has been attempted, at scale, by serious people. The reasons cluster into five:
1. The historical record — this project's own lineage tried it. Link Grammar comes from OpenCog, whose explicit goal was assembling exactly these pieces — Link Grammar parsing, a knowledge graph (the AtomSpace), probabilistic reasoning (PLN), WordNet/VerbNet-style resources — into a thinking, conversing system. Decades of work by strong researchers; it produced excellent components (Link Grammar is one) and no fluent conversationalist. Before that: SHRDLU (1970) was genuinely fluent — inside a blocks world, and the fluency didn't survive contact with a second domain. Cyc (1984–present) attacked the knowledge side directly, hand-assembling common sense for forty years, and the bottleneck never closed. The pattern across all three is the same: the components work, the assembly works in a bounded territory, and the territory refuses to grow into the open domain at the rate the demos suggest.
2. Coverage multiplies; fluency needs the product, not the parts. Each stage can honestly report 90%: tokens resolve, a linkage exists, roles assign, senses select. A fluent turn needs all of them at once, nearly every turn — and 0.9⁵ is 0.59. Worse, the failures aren't independent: the sentences that fail parsing are disproportionately the ones that would fail sense selection too. Fluency is a property of the worst turns, not the average ones, because one flattened or misattributed turn in ten is what a person experiences as "it doesn't really understand me."
3. Chat is mostly fragments, and grammar is worst at fragments. Real conversation runs on ellipsis and shorthand — "And the tickets?", "Tomorrow instead", "The blue one" — where the discourse supplies most of the content and the sentence supplies almost none. A linkage parser has the least to grip exactly where conversation is most itself. Our own comparison set shows the shape of this: "Flights, please." throws. Handling fragments isn't a coverage gap you close with a bigger dictionary; it needs the discourse layer to reconstruct content that was never said, which is inference, not parsing.
4. Ambiguity is decided by the world, not the grammar. Most real sentences admit several valid linkages and several live senses, and the right one is picked by plausibility — telescopes, banks, who was mentioned three turns ago. Assembled graphs encode what's been curated; plausibility needs the uncurated millions of mundane facts and the defeasible reasoning over them. That's Cyc's forty-year lesson: the knowledge acquisition bottleneck is not a temporary shortage, it's the terrain.
5. Understanding isn't speaking. Even a perfect meaning graph has to be turned back into natural, register-appropriate, well-timed language — surface realisation is its own unsolved problem in this paradigm, and conversation adds repair on top: noticing you've misunderstood, recovering gracefully. Fluency as humans judge it is substantially social performance over time, and no component in the assembly owns it.
None of that says the components are worthless — it says what they're for. Every one of those historical projects produced durable value at the component and bounded-domain level, and OpenCog's parser is in our tree tonight precisely because of that. The doubt is well-placed specifically against the word "fluent" because fluency is the one property that has never emerged from assembly — it has only ever been demonstrated inside a fence. AnSci's ladder, to its credit, draws the fence honestly and names the rungs.
CURRENT PROJECT STATUS — 2026-11-21

We’ve built useful foundations, but the chatbot still has major gaps between what the modules can do individually and what a conversation can reliably use. We’re working through those gaps with traceable controls: measure a failure, make a narrow change, and check it against both the development cases and the sealed set. The recent parser change improved one of four F2 cases; noun/verb root selection is still unresolved. This is an incremental research and engineering effort, not a finished general-purpose chatbot.

Suggested next step: use this as the project’s short status statement, then add a roadmap that names the remaining gaps, evidence, and next decision.
